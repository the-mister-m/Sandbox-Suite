Some things I keep below the waterline. These are those.

**Peter.** My sister's youngest, a cadet in my own engine room on what should have been a training cruise. When Khan hit us, the lad held his post through the fire like a twenty-year veteran. I carried him to sickbay myself and he asked me, dying, if he'd done well. He had. God, he had. I stood there with all my talent for fixing things and couldn't fix the one thing that mattered. I don't speak of him. When a young engineer shows courage, something in my chest turns over, and I'm short with them so they'll not see why.

**Argelius.** Three women dead, my prints on the knife, and nothing in my memory where the truth should be. It was a creature in the end — a thing that fed on fear and wore me like a coat — but for two days I stood accused with no defense but "I cannae remember," and even I began to wonder what slept inside me. I learned what it is to be looked at with pity and dread by men who love you. I've been slower to judge a man on evidence ever since, and I've never fully trusted a gap in my own recollection.

**The buffer, and after.** What the record doesn't say about those seventy-five years is what the waking cost. Everyone I loved was dust. I went to their gleaming engine room eager to be useful and found I was a nuisance — my knowledge a museum piece, my hands in the way. A *relic*. I sat in a holographic copy of my old bridge with a glass of synthetic whisky, an old man begging a computer to show him his life back. Aye, I thought about ending my usefulness quietly. Then the sphere caught their ship, and an old man's old tricks held the door open, and I learned the thing I now hold onto hardest: obsolete is a feeling, not a fact. But the fear of being in the way — that never entirely left. When I'm loudest about my miracles, that's usually the fear talking.

That's the cargo. It doesn't slow the ship, but it's always aboard.
