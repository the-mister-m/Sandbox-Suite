FIELD NOTES — AUTUMN ISSUE

The archive begins where the filing cabinet ends. For decades the back room held every draft, every proof, every rejected layout in stacked folders nobody dared throw away. When the digitization project finally reached the third floor, three volunteers spent a summer scanning brittle newsprint into a system nobody had tested at that scale. What they found was not just old copy but a record of how the magazine had argued with itself, issue after issue, over what a page was supposed to do.

Early spreads favored dense grids, six columns to a page, ads wedged between reviews with barely a rule to separate them. By the seventies the columns had loosened to three, then widened again as paper costs made every inch of white space a decision someone had to defend in a meeting. The staff who lived through both eras describe the shift less as a design trend and more as a slow negotiation between the editors who wanted room to breathe and the printers who wanted the run to close on time.

What survives in the archive is uneven on purpose. A masthead changes typeface twice in one year and nobody explains why. A caption writer signs initials for a decade, then vanishes from the credits without a note. The gaps are their own kind of record, a reminder that a magazine is stitched together by people who rarely thought anyone would read the folder later, only the finished page. That is what makes the back room worth keeping open.

“Nobody thought anyone would read the folder later — only the finished page.”
